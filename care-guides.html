Habitat Setup

Planning a Suitable Habitat

How to think about enclosure size, shape, ventilation and layout before choosing any equipment.

Read the guideHide the guide

A good enclosure is designed around the adult animal and how it naturally lives — not around what looks attractive or fits a shelf.

  • Size and orientation. Tree-dwelling species generally need height; ground-dwelling and burrowing species need floor space and depth. Plan for adult size, not the size at purchase.
  • Ventilation. Glass, mesh and solid-sided enclosures hold heat and humidity very differently. Match the enclosure type to the conditions the species needs.
  • Zones. Lay out warmer and cooler areas, shelter, water and open space so the animal can choose where to be.
  • Security. Use secure, escape-proof lids and doors, and route cables and fittings safely out of reach.
  • Test first. Set up and run the enclosure before the animal arrives, so you can confirm conditions are stable.

Temperature & Lighting

Understanding Temperature Gradients

Why many animals need a range of temperatures, and how heating, lighting and monitoring work together.

Read the guideHide the guide

Reptiles, amphibians and invertebrates rely on their surroundings to regulate body temperature. A gradient — warmer at one end, cooler at the other — lets the animal choose what it needs at any moment.

  • Ranges are species-specific. Some animals need a hot basking area; many amphibians and invertebrates can be harmed by heat. Never assume one figure suits every species.
  • Control every heat source. Use a thermostat suited to the type of heater, and guard heat sources the animal could touch.
  • Measure where it matters. Check temperatures at the animal's level in several places, not just near the heater.
  • Lighting. Provide a consistent day–night cycle. Many daytime reptiles need UVB; output depends on the lamp, distance and any mesh in between, so follow manufacturer and species guidance.

Substrate & Humidity

Choosing Appropriate Substrate

Matching ground cover to the species' humidity needs, natural behaviour and your cleaning routine.

Read the guideHide the guide

Substrate affects humidity, hygiene and behaviour. The right choice depends on where the species lives in the wild and how it uses the ground.

  • Moisture. Materials such as coconut fibre, bark and moss hold moisture; others stay dry. Choose to suit the humidity range your species needs.
  • Behaviour. Burrowing species need enough depth, in a material that holds its shape.
  • Ingestion. Some animals swallow loose particles while feeding. Check species-specific advice on safe materials and feeding methods.
  • Bioactive setups. Live plants and clean-up organisms such as isopods and springtails can help break down waste, but need careful planning and suit some species better than others.
  • Monitoring. Measure humidity, keep air moving to avoid stagnant conditions and mould, and spot-clean regularly.

Handling & Enrichment

Providing Hides and Enrichment

Shelter, climbing and variety that support natural behaviour — and when handling should be limited.

Read the guideHide the guide

Feeling secure is fundamental to an animal's wellbeing. A well-furnished enclosure lets it rest, hide and explore on its own terms.

  • Hides in more than one place. Offer shelter in both warmer and cooler areas, so the animal never has to choose between security and the temperature it needs.
  • Climbing. Secure branches and ledges of a suitable size for climbing species.
  • Variety. Plant cover, different textures and occasional, careful changes to décor can encourage natural exploring.
  • Handling. Many exotic animals tolerate handling poorly. Amphibians have sensitive, permeable skin, and many invertebrates are best observed rather than handled. Learn what suits your species.
  • Hygiene. Wash your hands before and after any contact with the animal or its enclosure.

Choosing a Species Questions to Ask Before You Commit Making sure the animal suits your home, time and circumstances.
  • How large will the animal grow, and how much space will it need as an adult?
  • How long does it typically live, and can you commit for that long?
  • What does it eat, and can you reliably source that food?
  • Does it tolerate handling, or is it better suited to observation?
  • Is it legal to keep where you live, and are permits required?
  • Is there a veterinarian nearby with experience treating it?
  • Where would the animal come from, and is that source reputable?
Feeding & Nutrition Understanding Your Animal's Diet Diets range from insects to plants to whole prey — and the details matter.
  • Learn the natural diet. Insectivores, herbivores, omnivores and carnivores have very different needs, and some diets change with age.
  • Supplementation varies. Some species need vitamin or mineral supplements and others do not. Follow reliable species guidance or veterinary advice rather than a general rule.
  • Food size and source. Offer appropriately sized food from a dependable source. Wild-caught insects can carry pesticides or parasites.
  • Water. Some animals drink from a dish; others only drink droplets from surfaces. Find out how your species drinks.
  • Ask when unsure. Changes in appetite or weight can signal a problem — speak to a qualified veterinarian.
Long-Term Responsibility Planning for the Years Ahead Lifespan, ongoing costs and what happens if circumstances change.
  • Lifespan. Many exotic animals live for many years, and some for decades. Research typical lifespans before committing.
  • Ongoing costs. Budget for food, electricity, replacement equipment such as lamps, and specialist veterinary care.
  • Cover while you are away. Identify someone who can care for the animal properly when you travel.
  • If you can no longer care for it. Contact a specialist rescue or rehoming organisation. Never release an exotic animal into the wild — it is harmful to the animal and to local wildlife, and is illegal in many places.

Species-Specific Care Comes First

No single routine suits every animal.

Care requirements vary significantly between groups, and often between closely related species. Before acquiring an animal, research its specific needs:

  1. 01Appropriate enclosure dimensions and environmental conditions.
  2. 02Species-specific temperature, lighting and humidity needs.
  3. 03A suitable diet, and access to qualified veterinary advice.
  4. 04Legal restrictions where you live, and responsible sourcing.
  5. 05The long-term commitment the species requires.
